Beyond Communication: Creating Ownership Through Leadership and Accountability

By Richard BuddSeptember 7, 202610 min read
LeadershipEmployee EngagementAccountability

In my last article, The Perception Gap, I explored how strategy, culture and values can lose clarity as they travel through an organisation. This article picks up the next question: even when employees understand the direction, what helps them feel personally responsible for helping the organisation get there?

The answer is not simply more updates or better-presented messages. It is helping people translate organisational ambition into choices they can make, problems they can solve and improvements they can influence through their own work.

That distinction matters because genuine engagement is built through ownership, not awareness alone. People are more likely to be motivated, innovative and committed when they are trusted to shape outcomes and can see the difference their contribution makes.

Performance Conversations Matter More Than Performance Reviews

One of the most common challenges I come across is organisations relying heavily on annual appraisal processes whilst wanting to create a culture of agility, accountability and continuous improvement.

The problem is that many appraisals have become more about process than performance. Forms are completed, ratings are discussed and objectives are reviewed, but too often the conversation feels like an administrative exercise rather than a meaningful discussion about contribution, development and future success.

I've seen organisations invest huge amounts of time preparing for annual reviews, only for employees to tell them they don't really know how they are performing or what is expected of them for the rest of the year.

The organisations that seem to gain the greatest benefit are those that focus less on annual events and more on regular conversations. Managers create time to discuss what success looks like, how individuals are contributing to wider business goals, where they want to develop and what support they need.

When performance conversations become a regular part of leadership rather than an annual process, employees gain far more clarity about their role and the impact they have on the business.

Some organisations have adopted quarterly goal-setting, sprint reviews, OKRs or EOS scorecards to support these conversations. The framework itself is less important than the discussion it creates. Done well, these approaches help people understand not only what they are doing, but why it matters.

From Being Told to Being Asked

Another theme I see regularly is that organisations say they want employees to take ownership, but many management approaches are still built around telling people what needs to be done rather than involving them in finding the solution.

This is often an untapped opportunity. The people closest to customers, processes and operational challenges frequently have the best understanding of what's working and what isn't. Yet many employees still feel their role is simply to execute decisions rather than influence them.

I recently came across an infographic comparing traditional leadership and servant leadership. Whilst it's a simplified model, I thought it captured an important point. Traditional leadership often places decision-making and control at the top of the organisation. Employees are expected to follow direction whilst managers oversee execution.

The servant leadership model turns that thinking on its head. The leader's role becomes creating clarity, removing obstacles and supporting others to succeed. Managers coach and develop people. Employees are trusted to make decisions and take ownership. Ultimately, the focus shifts towards creating value for customers through empowered teams.

Whether you subscribe fully to servant leadership or not, I think there is an important lesson here. If organisations genuinely want employees to take ownership, they have to be prepared to give them opportunities to influence outcomes.

The most effective leaders I've worked with tend to ask more questions than they provide answers. They ask:

  • What could we do better?
  • What are customers telling us?
  • What's slowing us down?
  • What opportunities are we missing?
  • If you were leading this area, what would you change?

These conversations do more than generate ideas. They create belief. Employees begin to see themselves as contributors to success rather than recipients of instructions.

Connecting Individual Roles to Organisational Success

The previous article focused on whether organisational messages retain their meaning as they move through different layers. The practical challenge here is what happens after the message has landed.

Employees may be able to describe the strategy and still be unsure which decisions, behaviours or priorities they personally need to change. Knowing the destination is not the same as knowing what responsibility you carry on the journey.

A useful test is whether people can answer a more demanding question: "What will I do differently because of the goals we are trying to achieve?"

If the answer is vague, the organisation has not yet converted direction into ownership.

Leaders and managers have to make that translation explicit: which outcomes matter most, what trade-offs may be needed, where people have room to decide and how progress will be reviewed.

One of the most effective ways I've seen organisations address this is by creating clear links between organisational objectives, team priorities and individual contribution.

Frameworks such as OKRs, EOS and business sprints can help because they make those links more visible. Employees can see how their objectives contribute to team performance, how team performance contributes to organisational success and where accountability sits.

When people understand the impact of their work, priorities become clearer, decision-making improves and engagement tends to increase because people can see that what they do genuinely matters.

Recognising More Than Results

Many organisations say they value innovation, collaboration and continuous improvement, but their reward and recognition systems often tell a different story.

People are recognised for hitting targets, delivering projects or achieving financial results, all of which are important. However, if these are the only things that receive attention, employees naturally focus on immediate outputs.

In my experience, organisations get much more value when they also recognise behaviours that support long-term success.

That might include introducing a new idea, improving a process, sharing expertise, mentoring colleagues, solving a recurring customer challenge or proactively developing new skills.

If businesses want employees to think beyond today's targets, they need to celebrate contributions that strengthen tomorrow's performance as well.

Building Capability for the Future

One of the strongest indicators that employees genuinely feel connected to the organisation's success is when they proactively invest in their own development.

I've worked with organisations where employees actively sought opportunities to learn because they understood where the business was heading and wanted to be part of that future. They weren't attending training because somebody told them to. They were doing it because they could see a connection between their development and the organisation's success.

This requires organisations to be open about future challenges, future opportunities and future capability requirements.

People are far more likely to take ownership of learning when they have a clear understanding of where the business is heading and how they can contribute to that journey.

The "What Happens If They Leave?" Test

A question I often ask leaders is: "If this person left tomorrow, what would we lose?"

Most organisations have individuals with specialist knowledge, unique expertise or critical relationships. In many cases, others don't fully appreciate the value of their contribution until they are no longer there.

That creates both a business continuity risk and an engagement opportunity.

Are people encouraged to share knowledge? Do colleagues understand how different roles contribute to success? Can employees clearly articulate the value they bring?

The organisations that do this well encourage knowledge sharing, collaboration and visibility. They help employees understand not only what they do, but why it matters and who benefits from it.

When people understand their contribution, they tend to feel more connected to the wider purpose of the organisation.

From Awareness to Ownership

Communication can open the door, but it cannot create ownership on its own.

Ownership grows when people understand the choices they can make, see how their work affects results and believe their judgement is trusted. That requires leaders to turn strategy into regular conversations, practical priorities and genuine opportunities to influence how work is done.

In my experience, the organisations that make the biggest strides are those that embed meaningful performance conversations, give employees a genuine voice, make accountability visible and recognise behaviours that build future capability as well as current results.

Perhaps most importantly, their leaders see their role not as directing every answer but as creating the conditions for others to succeed.

That is where servant leadership becomes practical rather than theoretical: leaders create clarity, remove barriers and give people the confidence and authority to contribute.

It's where employees stop feeling informed about success and start feeling responsible for it.
