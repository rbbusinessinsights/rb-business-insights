Why Most Organisations Are Chasing Employee Engagement the Wrong Way

By Richard BuddAugust 6, 20265 min read
LeadershipEmployee Engagement

Employee engagement has become one of the most talked about topics in organisational life, yet despite years of surveys, action plans, wellbeing initiatives and engagement programmes, many organisations still find themselves asking the same question:

Having worked with organisations across a wide range of sectors over the last two decades, I’ve noticed that the answer is often surprisingly simple.

Many organisations have become focused on measuring engagement rather than creating the conditions that allow engagement to thrive in the first place.

The result is that huge amounts of time, effort and resource are invested in initiatives designed to improve engagement scores, while the underlying experiences that shape how people feel about work often remain unchanged.

The irony is that most employees are rarely asking for engagement programmes. What they’re really asking for is trust, fairness, clarity, involvement and leadership they can believe in.

The Problem With Treating Engagement as a Metric

Most organisations start with good intentions. They conduct a survey, gather feedback, analyse the data and identify areas for improvement. On the surface this seems entirely sensible. After all, if you want to improve something, surely you need to measure it first.

The challenge comes when the score itself becomes the focus.

We’ve seen organisations celebrate a two-point increase in engagement while simultaneously facing growing frustration amongst employees. Equally, we’ve seen organisations become concerned about a disappointing survey result despite having strong relationships, committed teams and a positive culture.

The reality is that engagement is not a standalone outcome that can be managed directly. Rather, it is usually a reflection of the experience people have every day at work.

If employees trust their leaders, feel valued, understand how their role contributes to success and believe their voice matters, engagement tends to follow naturally. When those conditions are missing, no amount of measurement will compensate for the gap.

In that sense, engagement is often less like a target and more like a barometer. It tells you something about the environment people are experiencing, but improving the reading on the dial requires attention to the conditions underneath.

Employees Rarely Want More Communication

One of the most common themes that emerges during employee focus groups is communication. Many leaders hear this feedback and immediately conclude that employees want more communication from the business.

In our experience, that conclusion is often wrong.

Most employees are already overwhelmed with emails, meetings, Teams messages, newsletters and organisational updates. The issue is rarely the amount of communication. More often, it’s the quality and relevance of it.

People want to understand why decisions are being made. They want transparency when difficult changes are necessary. They want information that helps them make sense of what is happening around them and what it means for their role.

When organisations communicate frequently but fail to explain the thinking behind decisions, employees can leave feeling informed but not engaged. Over time that creates distance between leaders and employees, and once trust begins to erode, engagement often follows closely behind.

The Difference Between Listening and Being Heard

Another trap organisations fall into is confusing feedback collection with genuine listening.

Modern organisations are better equipped than ever to gather employee opinions. Surveys, pulse checks, suggestion platforms and workshops all provide opportunities for employees to share their views.

Yet many employees tell us the same thing: “We’ve given our feedback before and nothing happened.”

That single perception can be incredibly damaging. When people stop believing their voice leads to change, they gradually disengage from the process. Future surveys become exercises in compliance, feedback becomes less honest and leaders lose access to some of the most valuable insights available to them.

The organisations that create genuine engagement are not necessarily those that act on every suggestion. That’s neither practical nor realistic.

Instead, they are the organisations that close the loop. They demonstrate that feedback has been considered, explain what will happen next and are honest about what cannot be changed and why. People are far more likely to stay engaged when they believe their voice matters, even when they do not get the answer they hoped for.

Why Managers Matter More Than Any Initiative

Perhaps the biggest mistake organisations make is assuming engagement is created through programmes.

In reality, most employees experience their organisation through their immediate manager. They don’t judge culture based on the values statement on the website or the content of the latest leadership presentation. They judge it through everyday interactions.

Do I feel respected? Do I know what’s expected of me? Does my manager listen to me? Can I raise concerns safely? Does somebody notice when I’ve done a good job?

These seemingly small moments have a far greater influence on engagement than many large-scale organisational initiatives.

This is why organisations with excellent people strategies can still struggle to create highly engaged cultures. If employees don’t trust local leadership, the wider strategy often fails to translate into daily experience.

Conversely, we’ve seen managers create highly engaged teams even in organisations facing significant challenges, simply because people felt supported, valued and treated fairly.

The Question Leaders Should Really Be Asking

When leadership teams ask, “How can we improve employee engagement?”, they are often focusing on the symptom rather than the cause.

A more useful question is: “What is it actually like to work here?”

Not from the perspective of senior leaders, or through survey dashboards and performance reports, but from the perspective of the employee answering customer calls, leading a shift team, maintaining equipment, supporting colleagues or solving problems every day.

When leaders start to understand those everyday experiences, the conversation changes. The focus moves away from engagement initiatives and towards creating an environment where people can do their best work, feel respected and understand the contribution they make.

And in our experience, that’s where sustainable engagement is really built.

Creating the Conditions for Engagement

The organisations that achieve the strongest levels of engagement are rarely obsessed with engagement itself.

Instead, they focus relentlessly on trust, communication, leadership capability, fairness, involvement and employee experience. Engagement then becomes the natural outcome rather than the objective.

Because people don’t become engaged because an organisation asks them to be. They become engaged when the environment around them gives them good reasons to care.

And that’s a very different challenge altogether.
