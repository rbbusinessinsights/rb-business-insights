Why Most Organisations Are Chasing Employee Engagement the Wrong Way

By Richard Budd, RB Business Insights • August 6, 2026 • 5 min read
← Back to Resources

I've lost count of the number of employee focus groups I've facilitated over the years.

Different sectors. Different locations. Different leadership teams.

Yet the conversations often follow a remarkably similar pattern.

Senior leaders tell me they want to improve engagement.

Employees tell me they want to be listened to.

At first, those two things sound closely connected.

They're not.

Engagement Isn't a Survey Score

Too often, employee engagement becomes something organisations try to measure rather than something they try to understand.

A survey goes out.

A score comes back.

A presentation is created.

An action plan follows.

Everyone feels like progress has been made.

But six months later, many of the same frustrations remain.

Not because leaders don't care.

Usually because they've focused on the symptom rather than the cause.

Engagement isn't about how people answer a survey once a year.

It's about how they feel on a Tuesday morning when nobody is watching.

Do they feel trusted?

Do they feel their opinion matters?

Do they believe decisions are made for the right reasons?

Do they feel that somebody would notice if they weren't there tomorrow?

Those are the questions that really matter.

People Rarely Ask for More Communication

One of the biggest myths in business is that employees want more communication.

Most don't.

They want better communication.

There's a difference.

In organisations up and down the country, people are drowning in emails, Teams messages, newsletters and leadership updates.

The issue isn't volume.

The issue is relevance.

I've spoken to countless employees who can recite the latest corporate messages almost word for word, yet still have no real understanding of why important decisions have been made.

When people don't understand the "why", trust starts to erode.

And once trust disappears, engagement soon follows.

The Best Organisations Make People Feel Involved

A lot of leaders believe engagement is driven by perks, benefits and wellbeing initiatives.

Those things have value.

But they are rarely the reason people stay.

When employees tell me why they love working somewhere, the answers are surprisingly simple:

"I feel trusted."

"My manager genuinely cares."

"I know what's expected of me."

"I can influence things."

"People listen when I have an idea."

Notice what's missing.

Nobody talks about the fruit basket.

Nobody talks about the discount portal.

Nobody talks about free coffee.

People stay where they feel they matter.

Listening Isn't the Same as Hearing

Many organisations have become very good at collecting feedback.

Far fewer are good at acting on it.

Employees notice this quickly.

Nothing damages credibility faster than asking people for their views and then allowing those views to disappear into a black hole.

When that happens, future surveys become an exercise in compliance rather than honest feedback.

People stop telling you what they really think.

They start telling you what they think you want to hear.

And once that happens, leaders lose one of their most valuable sources of insight.

The organisations that build genuine engagement don't have all the answers.

They're simply better at showing people their voice has made a difference.

Leadership Matters More Than Any Initiative

Over the years I've reviewed organisations with impressive engagement strategies, sophisticated wellbeing programmes and excellent people policies.

Yet some of them still struggled to create highly engaged cultures.

Why?

Because people experience organisations through their managers.

Not through policy documents.

Not through PowerPoint presentations.

Not through values displayed on office walls.

Through daily interactions.

A manager who listens, gives clarity, follows through on commitments and treats people with respect will do more for engagement than any annual initiative ever could.

The opposite is equally true.

The most beautifully designed people strategy in the world will struggle if people don't trust the person they report to.

The Real Question Leaders Should Ask

Whenever I hear a leadership team ask:

"How can we improve engagement?"

I often think they're asking the wrong question.

A better question might be:

"What is it like to work here, really?"

Not in the boardroom.

Not in leadership meetings.

Not in reports and dashboards.

For the person answering customer calls.

For the team leader working nights.

For the engineer on site.

For the administrator keeping everything moving behind the scenes.

Because engagement isn't created through programmes.

It's created through everyday experiences.

Get those experiences right and engagement follows naturally.

Get them wrong and no survey score, wellbeing initiative or communication campaign will fix the problem.

In my experience, the organisations that truly thrive are not obsessed with measuring engagement.

They're obsessed with creating the conditions that allow it to exist.

And that's a very different conversation.
