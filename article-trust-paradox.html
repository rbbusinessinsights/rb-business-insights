The Trust Paradox

Why behaviour, not belief, determines trust and how leaders can bridge the trust gap to drive performance

🏢 Investors in People 📅 June 2026 ⏱ 15 min read

Leadership Culture Trust Thought Leadership
← Back to Resources

The Trust Challenge

At Investors in People, our purpose is simple: Make Work Better. And that starts with one thing - trust. It sounds easy, but as the research in this whitepaper shows, it's anything but.

Trust is the foundation of any organisation, the platform on which everything is built. It is the most powerful lever a leader has, the ultimate strategic enabler. With it, you drive engagement, ownership and buy-in from your people. Without it, all you have is a plan on a piece of paper.

The Paradox:

✓ 82% of HR leaders believe employees are trusted

✗ 61% of employees regularly "cover themselves"

✗ 65% avoid speaking openly at work

This research draws on how employees and HR leaders perceive and approach trust in the workplace. Leaders understand the value of trust, and there's considerable evidence linking it to stronger organisational performance. But how trust actually plays out day-to-day, how it shows up in the behaviour of employees and leaders, is far less clear.

What We Found

We set out to explore the story employee behaviour tells about trust today in organisations. You will find the full results throughout this paper, but in short, we found a clear rhetoric-reality gap between how people perceive trust and how they actually demonstrate it.

HR leaders may feel confident that their leaders are creating high trust cultures, but with employees routinely 'covering' themselves at work, hiding their mistakes and refusing to speak up about important issues, is that confidence misplaced?

With organisations chasing much-needed productivity gains in a complex and volatile environment, fixing this gap is critical. No one can afford the wasted energy employees appear to be spending on defensive behaviours, while innovation and growth cannot thrive sustainably in an environment where people are reluctant to speak up.

And because trust is dynamic, not static, the alignment between words and actions can quickly shift it – for better or for worse.

Why Trust Matters

In his landmark 2006 book The Speed of Trust, leadership guru Stephen Covey writes: "Low trust slows everything – every decision, every communication, and every relationship." It's a truism many HR professionals will likely be painfully aware of.

Research by neuroscientist Paul Zak, published in Harvard Business Review, lays out a business case for building trust at work that is hard to argue with. According to Zak's research, compared with people employed at low-trust companies, people in high-trust companies report:

Neuroscience Research Findings

  • 74% less stress
  • 106% more energy at work
  • 50% higher productivity
  • 13% fewer sick days
  • 76% more engagement
  • 40% less burnout

A recent CIPD evidence review on trust and psychological safety further confirms that trust is an organisational, not just cultural, priority. "Trust and psychological safety are linked to a wide range of positive outcomes," the report says. "These include individual attitudes, team behaviours and environment, and overall performance."

Understanding Trust

Trust at work is not a single thing. In this paper, we use trust to mean three distinct but connected concepts:

Three Dimensions of Trust

Trust is a feeling: The extent to which any individual feels safe and supported at work.

Trustworthiness: A characteristic of leaders and organisations. The qualities that make them deserving of trust – ability, integrity, benevolence and predictability.

Trusting behaviour: This is what people actually do when trust is present or absent – speaking up or staying silent, covering themselves or taking risks, committing or holding back.

While related and often interdependent, these three are not the same. Employees can feel broadly positive about their organisation and still exhibit defensive behaviours, for instance.

The Rhetoric-Reality Gap

When it comes to trust levels at work, our research – on the surface at least – seems relatively positive. Most HR leaders (82%) believe their people are completely or mostly trusted to use their judgement at work, and 72% of employees believe they are either completely or mostly trusted in their role.

A third (32%) of employees say that within their organisation people are trusted unless they give reason not to be. However, one in five people say trust depends heavily on who your manager happens to be, and one in 10 say trust at their organisation is talked about more than practised.

Dig a little deeper, however, and employee behaviour signals a trust paradox. The reality of day-to-day behaviour is not what you would expect from a high-trust culture and undermines the confidence HR professionals feel in how psychologically safe people feel at work.

Defensive behaviours are rife.

📊 61% of people admit to regularly 'covering' themselves at work (by keeping emails or screenshots)

📊 32% of employees do it most weeks

📊 Only 9% say they never cover themselves at work

📊 65% have avoided raising or speaking openly about something at work in the last six months

Key Insights for People Leaders

1. Trust impacts performance

Employees (93%) and HR leaders (100%) alike say trust is important at work. Trust is linked to performance, with 48% of HR leaders concerned that low trust in teams impacts productivity and 37% believing it leads to lower engagement.

2. There is a rhetoric-reality gap between trust and behaviours

While employee perceptions of trust within their organisations are relatively positive, this is undermined by ubiquitous defensive employee behaviours. Some 61% of people admit to regularly 'covering' themselves at work, with 32% doing it most weeks.

3. Employees are not speaking up

Around two-thirds (65%) of employees have avoided raising or speaking openly about something at work in the last six months, most commonly over concerns around workload or manager behaviour. A quarter of employees would try and fix a problem or mistake at work before telling anyone.

4. Low trust weakens commitment

One in five employees say low trust at work has weakened their commitment to their organisation. Low trust drives employees to disengage: three in 10 say it leads them to focus on protecting themselves, a quarter that they do only what is required and about a fifth that it would lead them to look for another job.

5. Managers influence trust

One in five employees say trust in their organisation depends heavily on who your manager is. The same proportion have felt reticent about speaking up about concerns over manager behaviour in their organisation.

6. Leaders aren't walking the walk

C-level leaders are more likely to cover up their mistakes, with a third (33%) not speaking up about mistakes they have made at work – more than twice as likely than the average employee. With role modelling and vulnerability a key part of building psychological safety, how many leaders are failing to walk the walk?

Bridging the Gap: Practical Recommendations

1. Measure, don't assume, trust

Trust is dynamic and lives through the everyday behaviours of the organisation. Measuring trust robustly is a prerequisite for improving it. Combine multiple data sources, including pulse surveys with questions designed to surface trust-related behaviours, exit interview analysis, and indicators like rates of escalation, speaking up and error reporting.

2. Equip managers to build trust

Managers have a huge impact on how people experience work day-to-day. Trust is no different, and as it is the foundation on which organisation performance is built, manager capability must be front and centre. Investing in manager development around empathy, self-awareness, conflict resolution and communication goes a long way.

3. Encourage leaders to role model vulnerability

Leaders are key to building psychological safety, but our data finds they are the most likely group to cover up their mistakes at work. Trust grows when leaders are willing to share their vulnerabilities and model openness and transparency about their own learnings.

4. Bake trust into HR processes

Trust must be baked into HR processes, with institutional trust as essential as leadership trust. This is even more pertinent in the age of AI, with many HR systems becoming increasingly automated. Systems and processes must be reviewed through a lens of fairness and trust.

5. Create the conditions for speaking up

Building a speak-up culture requires leaders to actively signal that challenge is welcome and demonstrate this through how they respond. Use a mix of channels such as anonymous feedback mechanisms, listening groups and skip-level meetings.

6. Involve people early in decision-making

Nothing erodes trust faster than decisions that feel imposed. Bringing employees into the conversation before decisions are final reduces self-orientation and builds shared ownership.

Expert Insight: The Operating Condition

"In organisations, trust isn't optional: it's foundational. It's the operating condition that makes high performance possible. Whether a team is high performing or not doesn't come down to capability or intelligence, it comes down to how people feel. When trust is present, you can quickly correct behaviour and steer people in the right direction. But when it isn't, even good decisions become difficult to land because there's a lack of understanding and trust in what you're trying to do."

— From Investors in People Research

Real-World Success Stories

BI Worldwide: Building Trust Through Employee Empowerment

When Andrea Rowe joined recognition, incentive and events company BI Worldwide as people and culture director, she found that trust and trust in leadership was low. She set out to fix it through a culture champions initiative, transparent CEO communications, revamped performance management processes, and affinity groups for psychological safety.

Results: Achieved Investors in People Gold accreditation, reduced attrition, decreased sickness absence, and increased customer NPS scores.

Fortem Solutions: Leadership Listening and Learning

At property solutions company Fortem, COO Chris Tredget made empowerment and trust central to leadership. He invested in leader development via personality profiling and 360-degree feedback, held inspirational strategy launch events, introduced monthly video updates, and created a trades group to gather frontline worker feedback.

Results: Attrition halved, profitability increased, engagement scores reached a 20-year high of 92%, and 81% of employees feel confident to challenge behaviours not aligned with company values.

Gunning London: Unlocking Psychological Safety

At construction firm Gunning London, people lead Gary Inman focused on consistency and systems. By providing clarity over job roles and workflows, building in consistent check-ins and dashboards, and focusing on process improvement rather than individual failure, the company built psychological safety.

Results: Improved bidding processes and continued business growth, with trust becoming a continuous and dynamic project.

The Path Forward

Building trust at work isn't about polished rhetoric or grand gestures, but about the behaviours that shape the everyday experiences of work. It lives or dies by how a leader responds when something goes wrong, whether people feel safe to raise concerns, whether what's promised is actually delivered.

Our research finds a gap in many organisations between the rhetoric around trust and how employees are showing up through their behaviours and in their relationships. This risks harming cultures and impacting performance and productivity, if employees are more focused on defensive behaviours like hiding their mistakes or concerns than contributing to the purpose and goals of the organisation.

Trust is dynamic not static. Building and maintaining it is an ongoing process, not a one-off event. It lives through human-to-human interactions, behaviours at all levels and in the fairness of institutional systems and processes.

HR professionals cannot own it, but they can contribute meaningfully to building the foundations that enable trust to grow, and through that drive sustainable, powerful organisational performance.
