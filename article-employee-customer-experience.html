Are We Missing the Connection Between Employee Experience and Customer Experience?

By RB Business InsightsNovember 3, 20267 min read
Employee ExperienceCustomer ExperienceCulture

I had an experience recently with a brand I use regularly that made me rethink something about my own work.

This was a brand I expected better from. It has a polished image and, from the outside at least, appears to take customer service seriously. But when something went wrong, I found myself dealing with barriers rather than anybody who seemed able or willing to resolve the problem. There was a definite sense of "computer says no". I couldn't get details of anyone more senior to escalate the issue to, and the responses felt so formulaic they could easily have been generated by AI.

What struck me wasn't simply that the service was poor. We have probably all experienced that at some point. It was the disconnect between the experience the brand appeared to promise and the experience its people and processes were actually delivering.

Much of my work focuses on the other side of that promise: the employee experience.

I spend time listening to people and exploring leadership, engagement, communication, development, recognition, organisational design and culture. Customer experience often comes into those conversations, but if I'm honest, I've probably tended to see it more as an outcome than something that should form an equal part of the work.

I'm beginning to think that needs to change.

Two separate journeys, but one organisation

The connection between employee experience and customer experience sounds obvious when you say it out loud or write it down. Yet how many businesses would put responsibility for both in one team?

Where responsibility sits in different teams, that isn't necessarily the result of a conscious decision to keep the two apart. It may simply be how the business has evolved, with each experience being approached as a separate discipline.

A customer experience specialist may be brought in to understand the customer journey, review touchpoints, analyse feedback and identify opportunities to improve service. Somewhere else, someone like me might be looking at the employee journey, engagement, leadership, culture, development and organisational design.

Both pieces of work can be valuable, but if they're happening independently, are we missing what connects them?

After all, the customer journey doesn't happen independently of the employee journey. At various points the two meet, and the quality of those interactions is influenced just as much by what is happening inside the organisation as it is by the customer service standards that have been set.

If we expect employees to listen carefully to customers, how well are we listening to employees? If we want people to take ownership of customer problems, have we given them the authority and confidence to do so? If we expect them to show empathy and make customers feel valued, are those behaviours reflected in how people are managed and treated internally?

That brings me back to the brand I mentioned at the start. Were the people I dealt with unwilling to help, or were their systems, policies and levels of authority making it difficult for them to do so? I couldn't tell. As a customer, I encountered barriers rather than the help I expected.

Why do we develop the two separately?

The connection isn't just about where responsibility sits. It also raises questions about how we train and develop people.

Think about the things we often include in customer service development: listening, empathy, understanding needs, communication, ownership, managing difficult conversations, building relationships and finding solutions.

How often do we place the same emphasis on demonstrating those behaviours internally?

If a manager listens properly to an employee who has a problem, that isn't so different from the behaviour we expect when dealing with a customer. If one department responds quickly and takes ownership of a request from another, isn't that part of the same service culture? And if passing a customer from one person to another without ownership is poor service, why do we sometimes accept exactly that behaviour between teams internally?

We could perhaps achieve much more by bringing employee and customer experience development together and creating one set of principles around how we treat people, whether they are customers, colleagues, suppliers or members of our own teams.

The same applies at a strategic level. If one team is designing the experience we want customers to receive while another is responsible for creating the experience of the employees expected to deliver it, surely those conversations need to be far more closely connected.

Are we investing in the wrong places?

Connecting the two experiences also means looking at where we invest.

Organisations spend significant amounts on marketing, business development, branding, CRM systems and technology designed to attract customers and create a great experience. We readily describe these things as investments because we expect them to contribute to growth.

Yet when margins become tighter, some of the first areas scrutinised can be employee costs, training, development, reward and headcount.

I've always found that slightly backwards and potentially very short-sighted.

We can invest heavily in creating a brand promise and persuading people to become customers, but somebody eventually has to deliver that promise. If the employees responsible for doing so are underdeveloped, overstretched, poorly managed or simply don't feel particularly valued themselves, there is a risk that all the investment made in attracting customers starts to unravel when they actually experience the organisation.

It's a little like investing in a fantastic car and then trying to save money by cutting back on servicing and maintenance. Eventually, neglecting what keeps everything performing is going to show.

Perhaps we need to become as comfortable talking about investment in the employee experience as we are talking about investment in the customer experience.

Maybe we need to look at the whole experience

The more I've thought about it, the more I've questioned whether it makes sense to develop employee and customer experience strategies separately and then look for ways to join them up.

What would happen if we mapped the customer and employee journeys alongside each other? I'd want to see where they meet and what employees experience behind the moments that matter most to customers. That would mean looking at whether people have the skills, confidence and authority to deliver what the brand promises, whether systems and processes help or hinder them, and whether the behaviours expected with customers are also how colleagues treat each other.

Looking at the two journeys together would bring customer experience, employee engagement, leadership, organisational design and development into the same conversation.

Looking back at the barriers and formulaic responses I encountered with that brand, I still don't know what was happening behind the scenes. What I can examine is how I approach these connections in my own work.

I've spent more than 20 years working across customer service, business development, people development and organisational improvement, yet in recent years I've probably discussed those experiences as though they belong in different boxes.

I'm increasingly convinced they don't.

When I next look at an employee's experience, I want to ask what it might mean for the customer on the other side of the conversation.

Perhaps the connection is obvious. The question is whether it is reflected in how we organise, develop and invest in our businesses.
