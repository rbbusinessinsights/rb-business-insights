The Perception Gap: What Gets Lost Between the Boardroom and the Front Line

By Richard Budd Sept 2, 2026 12 min read
Leadership Strategy Employee Engagement

One of the most revealing moments in any employee survey is not always the score itself. It is the distance between how senior leaders and front-line teams experience the same organisation. Leaders may feel clear on the vision, values and strategy, while employees closer to day-to-day delivery may experience something quite different.

Senior leaders may score highly when asked whether the organisation has a clear direction, strong values and a defined strategy. Ask front-line teams the same questions, and the results can look very different. Employees may be less clear about the organisation's direction, less connected to its priorities, and less convinced that their voices are being heard. It is tempting to jump to the conclusion that senior leaders are out of touch, but I think the reality is usually more nuanced than that.

In the organisations I have worked with, I have often seen senior leadership teams who genuinely care about their people. They spend time thinking about culture, they want the organisation to be clear about where it is heading, and they are usually trying to do the right thing. The difficulty is not always creating the strategy, values or direction. More often, it is helping those things stay meaningful as they travel from the boardroom to the front line.

The messages do not always disappear. They change.

Most organisations are not short of communication. They have leadership updates, team briefings, roadshows, intranet posts, staff newsletters, away days and manager cascades. The information is usually being shared. Yet somewhere between senior leaders setting direction and front-line employees experiencing the reality of work, something can shift.

Messages are interpreted through local pressures. Priorities become diluted by competing demands. Values start to rub up against targets, time pressures and customer issues. What starts as a clear message can end up feeling quite different to the people delivering services, supporting customers or keeping day-to-day operations moving.

The pressure in the middle

It is easy to point towards middle management, but I do not think blame helps very much. Middle managers often face pressure from every direction, trying to meet expectations from above while dealing with the realities and frustrations of their teams. They are balancing targets, customer demands, staffing issues, budgets, operational delivery and organisational change, often all at the same time.

In that environment, strategy can quickly become something people talk about when they have time, rather than something that genuinely shapes everyday decisions. Most managers are not deliberately blocking messages. Most are simply trying to keep everything moving. The risk is that important conversations about vision, culture and purpose are gradually squeezed out by the more immediate demands of the day.

Are managers being equipped for a changing world?

There is also a bigger question here. Have managers really been given the skills and support they need to operate the way work now does?

In many organisations, flexible and remote working has brought real benefits. But it has also changed how managers spot issues, build trust, notice changes in behaviour and understand what is really going on in their teams.

When people are less visible day-to-day, it can become easier for issues to go under the radar. People can hide, performance concerns can drift, wellbeing issues can get missed, and frustrations can quietly build in the background.

That does not mean flexible working is the problem. It means the role of the manager has become more complex, and organisations need to be honest about whether managers have been properly supported to adapt.

Managing in this environment requires different habits: more intentional check-ins, better listening skills, clearer expectations, confidence to have difficult conversations early and the ability to create connection even when people are not physically together every day.

If managers are expected to translate strategy, keep people connected, spot emerging issues and maintain performance in a changing world of work, then leadership development cannot be treated as a nice-to-have. It is one of the ways organisations close the perception gap before it widens.

This also links to Investors in People's "The Trust Paradox", which explores the gap between what leaders believe about trust and what people experience day-to-day. If people protect themselves, avoid difficult conversations, or choose not to speak openly, important issues can remain hidden until they become much harder to sort out.

Are leaders hearing the whole story?

The more uncomfortable question is whether senior leaders always get the full picture of what is happening. Again, I do not think this is usually about dishonesty. Most managers want to be seen as capable, and they want their teams, services and areas of responsibility to succeed.

As a result, problems can become softened. Concerns can be downplayed. Survey results can be explained away, and difficult issues can be presented as being under control. Not because managers are trying to mislead leaders, but because very few people want to appear to be failing.

The result can be an organisation where leaders genuinely believe communication is working, managers genuinely believe messages are being shared, and employees genuinely feel nobody is listening. All three perspectives can be true from where people are standing. And that is often where the perception gap begins.

Communication is not the same as understanding.

One of the biggest mistakes organisations make is measuring whether communication happened rather than whether understanding exists.

It is relatively easy to confirm that:

  • The presentation was delivered
  • The update was circulated
  • The briefing took place
  • The manager attended the session

What is much harder is establishing whether people can answer some very simple questions:

  • Do I understand where the organisation is going?
  • Do I know why that matters?
  • Do I understand how my role contributes?
  • Have I changed anything as a result?

If people cannot answer those questions, communication may have taken place, but connection has not.

Closing the gap through better conversations

If the perception gap is often created in the layers between senior leaders and front-line teams, then organisations need to create more opportunities for those layers to connect properly. That means relying less on traditional communication cascades alone, and more on genuine conversation, listening and reflection. One approach I have seen work well is for senior leaders to spend more time hearing directly from employees, rather than always through management structures.

Create direct routes to the front line

This is not about bypassing managers or undermining their role. It is about helping leaders stay close enough to understand how strategy, culture and organisational decisions are actually being experienced by the people doing the work every day. Regular skip-level meetings, where a manager's manager meets directly with front-line employees in small groups, can be helpful when they are handled well.

They create space for people to talk honestly about what is working well, what is creating frustration, what customers are experiencing and where organisational messages are not landing as intended. Just as importantly, they allow leaders to hear those perspectives without several layers of interpretation in between.

Get closer to operational reality

It is surprising how often senior leaders find themselves making decisions about areas they have not visited for months, and sometimes years. Whether it is spending time on the shop floor, observing customer interactions, joining operational meetings or simply shadowing employees for a few hours, leaders gain valuable insight when they see the organisation through the eyes of the people delivering the work.

Employees notice when leaders make that effort. It sends a powerful message that understanding everyday realities matters just as much as reviewing performance reports.

I saw this in a large organisation I worked with, where one senior leader made a deliberate effort to get out on the road and meet more than 750 people across his trades team.

On one of his early visits, he heard about a new policy that required trades teams to check the oil levels in their vans using a dipstick and record that the check had been done.

On the face of it, this was a fairly small task. But to the people being asked to do it, it was frustrating because they could not see the point. It was not something most people would routinely do in their own vehicle; servicing should reduce the chance of it becoming an issue, and the vehicle itself would probably flag a serious problem before it became critical.

My guess is that the policy was introduced somewhere in the business after one van developed an oil-related fault, resulting in an expensive repair. The intention was understandable. But the result was a process that felt out of proportion to the actual risk and, just as importantly, had not been properly explained to the people expected to do it.

That is exactly the type of insight leaders are unlikely to get from a dashboard. It comes from being close enough to hear not just what people are doing, but how decisions are actually landing with them.

Make dialogue part of the rhythm

Large-scale staff events have their place, but they rarely create genuine dialogue on their own. This links closely to the point I explored in last week's article on away days: the value is rarely in the slide deck or the perfectly worded strategy update. It is in the quality of the conversations, the questions people feel able to ask and the extent to which employees leave with a clearer understanding of where the organisation is heading.

The same principle applies here. If town halls become one-way broadcasts, they may inform people without really involving them. But when leaders create smaller, more human conversations alongside the larger events, they are far more likely to understand what is landing, what is being missed and where the message needs to be made clearer.

Small breakfast sessions, coffee mornings or informal roundtable discussions can often generate much richer feedback. Imagine a CEO hosting breakfast with eight front-line employees once a month: no presentations, no slides, just conversation. Over the course of a year, those discussions could provide a far better understanding of organisational culture than many formal reports.

Make employee voice meaningful

Many organisations have employee forums or employee voice groups, but they can easily become focused on operational irritations: the office temperature, the coffee machine, the parking situation. Those issues may matter to people, but they rarely help leaders understand whether the organisation's strategy, culture and values are actually being lived.

The most effective employee voice groups are connected to the organisation's real priorities. They explore whether people understand the strategy, whether values are visible in daily decisions, what barriers prevent people from delivering excellent service, where unnecessary frustration is being created, and what employees are hearing from customers.

Those conversations help organisations improve rather than simply react. They also help leaders see whether the messages they believe have landed are actually shaping how people think, decide and work.

Create a culture where bad news can travel upward

Perhaps the biggest challenge of all is creating an environment where people feel safe enough to speak honestly.

Most organisations say they welcome challenge. The real test is what happens when somebody raises something uncomfortable, inconvenient or hard to hear.

If managers believe that raising problems will damage their reputation, issues will inevitably be softened as they move up the chain. Leaders may receive a more positive picture than employees are experiencing.

The organisations with the smallest perception gaps are often those where managers and employees feel able to say, "This is not working", "People are confused" or "The strategy is not landing here", without fear of being blamed for saying it out loud.

Final thought

The perception gap isn't really a battle between senior leaders and front-line employees. Perhaps it is what happens, often unintentionally, when culture, values and strategy lose clarity as they travel through an organisation.

The real question for leaders is not just whether the message has been communicated. It is whether people have understood it, believed it and felt able to act differently because of it.

Because ultimately, organisations improve when reality is allowed to travel upwards just as clearly as strategy is expected to travel downwards.
